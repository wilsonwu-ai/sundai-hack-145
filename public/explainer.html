ChipletChill · Sundai Hack 145 · Track 2

A 2035 GPU package that runs 14.9 °C cooler, found in your browser

The hottest spot drops from 89.3 °C to 74.4 °C on the training workload, using 150 full solves. A solver-only search given 10× as many solves (1,500) does worse. The wire budget stays within 1.3× of the default layout.

The simple version

Four burners under one pan

  1. 1Picture one big frying pan sitting on four hot burners.
  2. 2The pan spreads the heat sideways, so each burner makes a warm glow around itself.
  3. 3Put the burners close together and the glows pile up. You get one scorching spot in the middle.
  4. 4Move the burners apart and the spot cools down, because each glow has room to fade.
  5. 5But each burner is tied to its neighbours with a short string (a wire). Strings only stretch so far, so you cannot shove burners to the corners. The game is to find the best spots the strings allow.

The burners are chiplets (small chips that sit together in one package). The pan is the lid and silicon. The strings are the links between chips.

The situation

What the hack asked

Track 1 asked teams to design the most cost-effective factory that turns energy into AI tokens (the small pieces of text a model reads and writes). The site could be space, Mars, Antarctica, underground or Cambridge. The deliverable is a digital twin (a computer model of the whole plant) covering power, computing and cooling, tuned to use the least energy on a set of typical 2035 prompts.

Track 2 asked for the coolest chiplet layout. Teams train a surrogate model (a fast stand-in for a slow simulator), then use it in a loop to arrange chiplets so the maximum temperature is as low as possible under set workloads. We picked Track 2.

Why chips run hot by 2035

Power per package keeps climbing. These are the figures we found:

  • AMD MI300X: 750 W; MI355X: 1,400 W (Wikipedia, AMD Instinct).
  • NVIDIA DGX B200: about 14.3 kW for 8 GPUs (NVIDIA).
  • Reported roadmap TDPs (thermal design power, the heat a chip is built to shed), second-hand and unverified: Rubin 1,800 W, Rubin Ultra 3,600 W with 12 to 16 HBM stacks (3DTested). HBM is high-bandwidth memory stacked next to the compute.
  • Rubin uses direct-to-chip liquid cooling (Introl, Jan 2026).

Our test package is 4 compute dies at 300 W, 4 HBM stacks at 30 W and one IO die at 40 W: 1.36 kW on a 60 × 60 mm base, with a cold plate and 35 °C coolant. That is a 2035 projection, not a product spec.

Picture 1

Heat piles up, then spreads out

Left alone, the four compute dies share one hotspot. Spread them as far as the links allow and each glow fades before it meets the next. The peak in the label is the default layout from our solver; the final label is the optimizer's result (74.4 °C).

How the search works

A slow judge and a fast guesser

The full solver is the ground truth. It models the package as a 2D plate (finite volumes, a way of chopping the plate into small cells, solved with conjugate gradients, an iterative method). It takes about 3 ms per solve on a 48×48 grid, which is too slow to try thousands of layouts. So we train a surrogate that guesses the peak temperature in well under a millisecond. The search runs on the surrogate. The solver only checks the best few.

Why does the surrogate work with so little data? Heat adds up: double the power and the temperature rise doubles. So each chiplet is a "glow" that fades over about 6.7 mm, plus mirror images at the package edges (heat cannot leave through the sides). Temperature is roughly the sum of the glows. The model only learns the weights.

One rule keeps it honest. Total link length may not exceed 1.3× the default layout's. Without it, the best answer would be to fling the hot dies to the corners.

Honest limits

What this does not show

  • It is a 2D plate, not a 3D stack. It captures sideways spreading and hotspot coupling, which is what layout changes.
  • HBM has its own temperature limit. We do not model it. The usual placement-paper threshold is about 85 °C, and per-stack HBM power is unsourced.
  • The split of watts between compute and memory for each workload is our assumption. The factors encode the direction only (decode is memory-bound), not measured watts.
  • The cold plate coefficient (20,000 W/m²K) and the lid and silicon conductivities are plausible textbook choices, not sourced figures.
  • All power numbers are 2035 projections. About 153 W/cm² per compute die is aggressive for today and plausible for 2035.

Prior art

Others got here first

  • TACPAs (2025): a neural-network surrogate plus annealing. Reported multi-GPU 95.27 to 91.33 °C. It uses the same loop shape as ours.
  • DiffChip (2025): a differentiable solver. Reported 85.98 to 75.44 °C.
  • RLPlanner (2023): reinforcement learning with a fast thermal model, 20.28% better objective than annealing with HotSpot (a standard thermal simulator).
  • TAP-2.5D (2021): first open-source 2.5D thermal placer, with HotSpot inside the loop.
  • DeepOHeat (2023): operator learning for full-chip thermal fields, 1,000× to 300,000× faster than Cadence Celsius.

How we differ: the whole loop runs in a browser tab in seconds, and the wire length is a hard limit, not a weighted term. Their reported numbers come from their own setups and are not comparable to ours.
